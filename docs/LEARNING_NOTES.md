# Learning Notes

Plain-language explanations of the concepts as they exist in this codebase, not in the abstract.

## Where `qpos`/`qvel` enter observations
MuJoCo stores the *entire* simulation state in two flat arrays: `data.qpos` (all positions -- 6DOF floating base + all 29 joint angles) and `data.qvel` (all velocities). `control/joint_map.build_joint_map` figures out, once, exactly which indices of those arrays belong to our 12 controlled leg joints (`qpos_adr`, `qvel_adr`), by asking MuJoCo itself (`mj_name2id`, `jnt_qposadr`, `jnt_dofadr`) rather than assuming a fixed layout. `envs/observations.build_observation` then slices out just those 12+12 values (relative to a default pose for position) as the joint part of the observation -- the other 24 joints (waist/arms) and the 7 floating-base values are never shown to the policy directly.

## Action -> target joint position -> PD/actuator -> torque
The policy doesn't output torque directly. It outputs a number in `[-1, 1]` per leg joint (`control/pd_controller.action_to_target`), which becomes `target_q = q_default + 0.25 * action` (clipped to the joint's real range). Then a PD law (`pd_torque`) computes `torque = kp*(target_q - q) - kd*qd` -- a spring pulling the joint toward the target, damped by velocity. This two-step indirection (position-target, not torque, as the policy's output) is standard in legged-robot RL: it's a smoother, more stable action space than raw torque, and it's also literally what a real position-controlled servo motor does.

## Physics dt vs. control dt
The simulator advances physics every `0.002s` (500Hz) -- fine enough for stable contact dynamics. But the policy doesn't get to act every physics step; it picks one action, which gets held for `control_decimation=10` physics steps (`0.02s`, 50Hz) before the policy sees a new observation and picks again (`envs/g1_walk_env.py:step`, the inner `for _ in range(control_decimation)` loop). This is decimation: the policy operates at a coarser, more tractable timescale, while the physics underneath still resolves contacts finely. Note the PD torque is *recomputed every physics substep* even though the target is fixed for all 10 -- because `q`/`qd` (the robot's actual state) keep changing within those 10 steps.

## Projected gravity / reference frames
"Projected gravity" answers: *which way is down, from the robot's own point of view?* We take the world's gravity direction `[0,0,-1]` and rotate it into the pelvis's own coordinate frame (`envs/observations.projected_gravity`, using the pelvis's rotation matrix, `data.xmat`). When upright, this is exactly `[0,0,-1]` (verified directly against the model). When the robot tips over, this vector rotates away from `[0,0,-1]` -- which is exactly how `envs/termination.py` detects a fall by *tilt* (as opposed to a fall by *height*): the z-component crossing a threshold means the robot's "down" no longer matches gravity's real "down." This is also why the *policy* can be given this signal even though it has no external camera: it's derivable purely from the robot's own internal orientation sensor (IMU), which is realistic for a real robot too.

## Return / value / advantage / GAE
The **return** is the sum of (discounted) future rewards from a given point in an episode. The **value function** V(s) is the policy's own *estimate* of that return, learned alongside the policy (shares no weights here, but is trained jointly by PPO). The **advantage** A(s,a) = "how much better was this specific action than the value function's average expectation for this state" -- and GAE (Generalized Advantage Estimation, Schulman et al. 2015) is a specific, tunable way to estimate that advantage from a rollout that trades off bias vs. variance (`gae_lambda=0.95` in our config; `gamma=0.99` is the discount factor). We never implement any of this math ourselves -- `stable_baselines3.PPO` does it internally every time `.learn()` is called; our job was only to supply `n_steps`, `gamma`, `gae_lambda` and a correct environment.

## PPO's old-policy ratio and clipping
Each PPO update reuses the *same* batch of rollout data for several epochs (`n_epochs=10`), which risks the policy drifting so far from the one that *collected* the data that the advantage estimates become invalid. PPO's fix: compute the probability ratio `r = pi_new(a|s) / pi_old(a|s)` for each action, and clip the objective so that `r` isn't allowed to move the policy's estimated improvement past `1 +/- clip_range` (0.2 here) worth of credit -- large policy changes are prevented even if the raw estimated advantage would reward them. `train/clip_fraction` in our logs is literally "what fraction of samples in this batch got clipped" -- ours ran unusually high (0.55-0.75, see `docs/FAILURE_ANALYSIS.md` F-0002), meaning most samples *were* being clipped, i.e. the policy wanted to move much further than the trust region allowed every single update.

## Why each reward term exists
- `tracking_lin_vel`: the actual task -- reward is highest (1.0, before the weight) when the robot's real base velocity exactly matches the commanded one, falling off smoothly (Gaussian-shaped, via `tracking_sigma`) as it misses.
- `alive_bonus`: a constant per-step reward for not having terminated yet. Without this, an early policy that falls in 1 step and one that survives 100 steps chasing bad velocity tracking could score similarly poorly; this cheaply rewards "still upright" as a distinct, easy-to-discover intermediate goal before precise tracking is learned.
- `torque_penalty`: a small quadratic penalty on total torque, discouraging needlessly stiff/high-effort motion (both more realistic and a small step toward eventual hardware safety), scaled deliberately small (`0.0002`) so it doesn't compete with `tracking_lin_vel` for priority (see `tests/test_reward.py::test_no_single_component_dominates_at_typical_scales`).

## Why each termination condition exists
`fell_height` (pelvis < 0.5m) and `fell_tilt` (projected gravity z > -0.3, i.e. tipped roughly beyond 70deg from upright) both mean "the robot has fallen" through two independent, cheap-to-compute signals -- catching both a straight-down collapse and a sideways/forward toppling that might not drop the pelvis height as fast. `numerical_invalid` (NaN/Inf in qpos/qvel) catches simulator blow-ups before they'd otherwise silently corrupt a rollout. All three end the episode immediately (`terminated=True`, value bootstrapping stops) rather than being folded into the reward, since "did it fall" is a much cleaner, better-defined signal as a hard episode boundary than as a penalty term.

## Train vs. deterministic play
During training, the policy samples actions from its (Gaussian) action distribution -- exploration is the point, so the same state can produce different actions on different attempts. `evaluation/evaluate.py` and `evaluation/record_video.py` instead call `model.predict(obs, deterministic=True)`, which always takes the distribution's mean (no sampling) -- this is "what does the policy actually think is best," used to measure real performance and to produce the reported curves/video, kept in a completely separate code path and a disjoint set of seeds (1000-1019) from any seed ever used in training, so a lucky training seed can't be mistaken for real generalization.
