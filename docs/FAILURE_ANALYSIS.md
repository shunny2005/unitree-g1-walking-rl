### F-0001 — `joint_map.build_joint_map` motor-vs-position check used the wrong MuJoCo field (caught pre-training, not a training failure)
Run(s): caught while writing `tests/test_joint_map.py` (2026-09-18), before any real training run depended on it.

Observed behavior: `control/joint_map.py` validated that each leg actuator was a direct-torque `<motor>` (not the upstream `<position>`) by checking `model.actuator_gaintype[aid] != mjGAIN_FIXED`. This check could never actually fail, for either actuator type.

Quantitative evidence: direct inspection via `mj_name2id`/`model.actuator_gaintype`/`model.actuator_biastype` on both `assets/g1/scene.xml` (upstream, `<position>`) and `assets/g1/scene_walk.xml` (derived, `<motor>`) for `left_hip_pitch_joint`: both report `gaintype=0` (FIXED). They differ only in `biastype`: `1` (AFFINE, encodes the position controller's kp/kv as bias terms) for `<position>`, `0` (NONE) for `<motor>`.

Likely mechanism(s): MuJoCo's `<position>` actuator implements PD control via the *bias* term (`biastype=affine`, using `gainprm`/`biasprm` together), not via `gaintype`. `gaintype=FIXED` is the default for ordinary force-scaling and is shared by both actuator classes. Assuming `gaintype` encoded "is this a motor" was an unverified guess about MuJoCo's actuator model.

Competing explanations: none needed -- confirmed directly by reading both fields off the live model rather than guessing from documentation.

Fastest discriminating test: `tests/test_joint_map.py::test_rejects_upstream_model_with_position_actuators` -- calling `build_joint_map` on the upstream (`<position>`) model must raise `JointMapError`; it did not, until the check was switched to `actuator_biastype != mjBIAS_NONE`.

Result: fixed in `control/joint_map.py` (now checks `biastype`); `tests/test_joint_map.py` covers both the positive case (`g1_walk.xml` passes) and the negative case (`g1.xml` is rejected). All 27 unit tests pass after the fix.

Conclusion: this validation gate was silently inert (would accept either actuator type) but never caused a wrong-torque bug in practice, because `assets/g1/g1_walk.xml` was in fact always the model we loaded end-to-end (never accidentally ran the pipeline against upstream `g1.xml`). Recorded because it's exactly the class of mistake CLAUDE.md warns about ("never guess joint ordering/actuator mapping when it can be verified from model data") and because the fix changes what future model-derivation bugs this test will actually catch.

Follow-up: none needed beyond the fix already applied. General lesson applied going forward: any MuJoCo model-introspection assumption written in this project should be checked against `model.*` fields directly (as done here) before being relied on in a validation gate.

### F-0002 — `baseline0001` falls mostly (not only) at higher commanded speed; PPO updates run far hotter than typical targets
Run(s): EXP-0005 (`experiments/runs/baseline0001`), deterministic eval over seeds 1000-1019.

Observed behavior: 15/20 deterministic-eval episodes survive the full 20s with good velocity tracking (0.06-0.16 m/s error); the other 5 fall (`fell_height`). Separately, `train/approx_kl` sat at 0.09-0.19 and `train/clip_fraction` at 0.55-0.75 for most of training -- both far above commonly-targeted PPO ranges (~0.01-0.03 KL is typical guidance from the PPO paper/Spinning Up).

Quantitative evidence: the 5 falling episodes' commanded speed magnitudes (`sqrt(vx^2+vy^2)`): seeds 1013 (0.51), 1017 (0.57), 1001 (0.42) are all clearly above the entire surviving-episode range (0.06-0.36); these 3/5 support a speed-threshold story. The other 2/5 (seed 1005 at 0.18, seed 1007 at 0.13) fall *inside* the bulk of the surviving-episode speed distribution and fall almost immediately (3.1s and 5.0s) despite several survivors commanding comparable or higher speed -- **this is not explained by commanded speed alone** (caught by an independent audit, see docs/AUDIT.md F4; correcting an earlier overstatement here that called the pattern "clean"). The honest read: high commanded speed is *a* contributor (3/5 falls), but at least 2/5 falls have some other seed-specific cause (e.g. a particular initial-condition/lateral-command combination triggering instability) that this analysis has not isolated. Training curve (`curves.png`) shows two clear regressions (dips) in `ep_len_mean`/`ep_rew_mean` during the 2.0M-3.0M timestep plateau, consistent with large, destabilizing policy updates rather than smooth convergence.

Likely mechanism(s): (1) the policy has not yet learned a gait robust to the fast end of the commanded-velocity range -- plausible simply from less training-time exposure/harder dynamics at high speed; (2) separately, PPO's default `clip_range=0.2` with no `target_kl` early-stopping, at `batch_size=256` over `n_steps=1024 * n_envs=12 = 12288` samples per rollout (48 minibatches x `n_epochs=10` = 480 gradient steps per iteration), is allowing very large policy shifts per iteration, which could be actively overwriting a working slower-speed solution while attempting to improve the faster-speed case (or vice versa) -- this is a plausible contributor to the plateau's oscillation, not confirmed as the cause of the high-speed falls specifically.

Competing explanations:
(a) high-speed falls are purely a "not enough training at that regime" issue -- more budget alone would fix it, independent of PPO update aggressiveness;
(b) the high KL/clip-fraction is destabilizing training broadly (visible in the plateau oscillation) and is also indirectly responsible for the high-speed failures (a stable update process might consolidate a faster gait rather than losing it to noise);
(c) the *reward* itself under-penalizes fast-speed instability (e.g. `tracking_sigma=0.25` gives a broad tolerance band that doesn't clearly reward the exact high-speed gait needed) -- a reward-shaping explanation rather than an optimization one.

Fastest discriminating test: rerun the same env/reward/budget with only the PPO update-aggressiveness reduced (lower `clip_range`, add `target_kl`) and compare deterministic-eval survival-vs-speed pattern against `baseline0001` on the *same* eval seeds. If the high-speed falls persist identically, (a) or (c) is favored over (b); if they improve, (b) is supported.

Result: EXP-0006 (`target_kl=0.03`, same budget/seed/env count as `baseline0001`) capped `approx_kl` to 0.02-0.04 and `clip_fraction` to ~0.25-0.29 (vs. baseline's 0.09-0.19 / 0.55-0.75) -- the intervention worked exactly as intended on the PPO-internals level. Deterministic eval (same 20 fixed seeds): **20/20 episodes now survive the full 20s (0 falls, vs. baseline's 15/20)**, and mean total reward is nearly identical to baseline (952.0 vs. 947.1). Read naively, this looks like an unambiguous win.

It is not. Adding a **distance-traveled** metric (net pelvis xy displacement over the episode, vs. the commanded displacement) to `evaluation/evaluate.py` after this result looked suspicious revealed: `baseline0001` covers a mean **1.85m** per episode (56.6% of the commanded expected distance) -- genuine, if imperfect, forward locomotion. `exp0006_target_kl` covers a mean **0.21m** per episode (5.1% of expected) -- despite never falling, it is essentially standing/marching in place, not walking. Its velocity-tracking error is also *worse* (0.225 vs. baseline's 0.155 m/s), consistent with barely moving rather than tracking well. The near-identical total reward across both runs is explained by `alive_bonus` dominating the reward for a policy that survives the full episode almost motionless, combined with a low torque penalty from producing very little motion -- neither of which required real walking to obtain.

Conclusion: capping PPO's update aggressiveness (`target_kl`) did not fix the high-speed-command failure by producing a *more capable* gait -- it found a degenerate, low-effort local optimum (near-stillness) that trivially avoids the fall-termination conditions instead. This is a legitimate PPO/reward-interaction finding, not the hoped-for fix: the original hypothesis (b) from F-0002 ("stable updates -> better high-speed gait") is **not supported**; a variant of hypothesis (c) is closer to correct -- the reward as currently weighted (`alive_bonus=0.5` fixed per step, `tracking_lin_vel` with a forgiving `tracking_sigma=0.25`) does not sufficiently penalize "surviving without actually walking," and a training process with enough freedom to explore (baseline0001's higher-KL, noisier updates) happened to find real walking instead of the safer degenerate solution -- not because instability was good, but because the reward alone doesn't rule out standing-still as attractive once the policy could reach it.

**`baseline0001` remains the canonical baseline** -- it is the genuinely better walker by the metric that actually matters (distance traveled), despite its higher-speed-command failures. EXP-0006 is kept as a documented, informative negative result, not reverted-and-forgotten.

Methodological lesson (applied going forward, and to any future evaluation in this project): **total reward and survival/episode-length are not sufficient evidence that a policy is walking** -- `alive_bonus`-style terms make "not falling" cheap to satisfy without real progress. `evaluation/evaluate.py` now always reports `distance_traveled_m` / `distance_ratio` alongside reward and survival for exactly this reason (CLAUDE.md: "do not say improved without naming a comparable metric/protocol" -- reward and survival alone were not that metric here).

**Third occurrence (2026-09-19, EXP-0022, `docs/DECISIONS.md` ADR-010 addendum):** the same degenerate "alive but not walking" optimum reappeared in a very different setting -- training the project's much richer, later-stage reward (`feet_air_time_bonus=6.0` + `heading_deviation_penalty=0.5` on top of the original three terms) entirely from scratch, with standard uncapped PPO (no `target_kl`, unlike EXP-0006's mechanism). `approx_kl` ran persistently high (0.09-0.21, never settling) for the full 20M-timestep run -- the opposite training-dynamics signature from EXP-0006's capped-KL cause -- yet the outcome was the same: every checkpoint stood in place (final pelvis height ~0.78, 0-3/20 falls) while translating only 0.2-0.4m over a full 20-second episode (`dist_ratio` 0.04-0.12 across the whole trend, no improving trend with more budget). This shows the mechanism is broader than "KL-capping suppresses the exploration needed to find real walking" (EXP-0006's specific story) -- **a sufficiently rich multi-term reward can make "survive without moving" a strongly-attracting local optimum from a random start regardless of how much exploration noise PPO has**, high-KL included. The same reward is not hard to reach at all when approached incrementally from a policy that already walks (that is exactly what the project's sequential fine-tuning chain, EXP-0016 through EXP-0020, did successfully) -- so the practical lesson is that curriculum order/starting point matters at least as much as reward-shape correctness once a reward has several interacting terms. Full writeup: `experiments/EXPERIMENT_LOG.md` EXP-0022.

**What actually causes the oscillation, isolated (2026-09-19, EXP-0023):** EXP-0022 conflated two variables at once (reward richness and learning rate), so it couldn't say which one caused the collapse. EXP-0023 isolates LR alone -- `config/default.yaml`'s exact minimal reward (same as `baseline0001`/`exp0009`), random initialization (no warm start), but `learning_rate=5e-5` instead of the standard 3e-4. Result: `approx_kl` never exceeded 0.061 across the full 10M-timestep run (vs. `baseline0001`/`exp0009`'s typical 0.09-0.19), the training curve is smooth and essentially monotonic (largest dip was 16.8%, occurring at a near-zero reward level early in training, not a late climb-crash-recover cycle), and the resulting checkpoint reached `dist_ratio=0.848` -- the best pure-tracking result in the project (canonical `exp0017`: 0.709). **Low LR alone, independent of warm-starting, is sufficient to avoid the from-scratch PPO oscillation.** This resolves the question PROJECT_STATE.md had flagged as the project's last genuinely open item.

This came with a real caveat, though, caught only by the controlled gait diagnostic (`diagnostics/gait_diagnostic.py`), not by any aggregate metric: the resulting gait has a foot-lift amplitude of only 1.2cm (visibly flatter than canonical's already-modest 3cm range) at a much higher stepping frequency -- a small, rapid shuffle, essentially reproducing the *exact* stepping problem F-0003/F-0004 were built to fix. This makes sense in hindsight: this run's reward has no `feet_air_time_bonus` term, so nothing in the objective asks for a longer stride regardless of how stably it's optimized. The lesson generalizes: **low LR fixes how reliably PPO reaches a good optimum for whatever the reward specifies; it does not change what that optimum is.** A smooth, well-converged training run and a *good* gait are different claims, and this project's own diagnostic tooling (built for exactly this reason, F-0003) was what caught the difference here too.

**Fourth occurrence, and the decisive one (2026-09-19, EXP-0024):** the clean follow-up EXP-0023 motivated -- the *full* rich reward (`feet_air_time_bonus=6.0` + `heading_deviation_penalty=0.5`), from scratch, at this same low LR (5e-5) -- collapsed even more completely than EXP-0022's standard-LR attempt. `dist_ratio` stayed at 0.014-0.032 across the entire 20M-timestep trend (worse than EXP-0022's 0.04-0.12), and direct inspection showed `distance_traveled_m` of only 0.04-0.05m *over an entire 20-second episode* with yaw drift of just 0.17-0.75 deg/10s -- an essentially perfectly frozen stance, not even the mild in-place swaying EXP-0022's policy showed. `approx_kl` this time was moderate (0.033-0.045, between EXP-0023's <0.03 and EXP-0022's 0.09-0.21) -- notably, a *more* stable, lower-variance optimizer produced a *more* complete collapse, not a less complete one. This decisively answers the question EXP-0022 left open: **learning rate is not the operative variable in this failure mode.** The mechanism is the reward's own local-optimum structure: `heading_deviation_penalty`, `ang_vel_penalty`, `torque_penalty`, and `action_rate_penalty` are all trivially and perfectly minimized by never moving or rotating at all, making "stand still" a strongly-attracting optimum from a random start regardless of how that start is explored -- a noisy search and a careful, low-variance one both find the same nearby trap. **Across every learning rate tested, the only method that reliably avoids it is starting from a policy that is already far from that basin (warm-starting from an established walker) -- this is not a convenient shortcut, it is the specific thing that makes this reward trainable at all.** Full writeup: `experiments/EXPERIMENT_LOG.md` EXP-0024, `docs/DECISIONS.md` ADR-010 addendum.

Follow-up (superseded by F-0003 below, which found two more specific gait-quality problems not visible in the aggregate metrics used so far): EXP-0007 (reduce `alive_bonus` 0.5->0.1, isolated) and EXP-0008 (combine with `target_kl`) both ran. Neither confirmed hypothesis (c) either -- see **Resolution** below.

---

**Resolution (2026-09-18, after EXP-0007/0008/0009):** the reward-shaping hypothesis (c) above turned out to be wrong too. EXP-0007 (lower `alive_bonus` alone) made `dist_ratio` *worse* (0.337 vs. baseline's 0.479) and increased falls (6/20). EXP-0008 (lower `alive_bonus` + `target_kl` together) was far worse still (20/20 falls, mean episode length 2.4s) -- capping update aggressiveness while also removing the easy `alive_bonus` reward signal left the optimizer with a harder landscape and a slower update rule, and it simply didn't have enough budget (still 3M timesteps) to recover.

That last observation was the actual clue: **every experiment so far (EXP-0006/0007/0008) held the training budget fixed at 3M timesteps and varied config.** EXP-0009 instead held config fixed (back to `baseline0001`'s exact, unmodified settings) and varied only budget, extending to 10M timesteps with checkpoints every 2M. Every checkpoint from 4M onward already beat `baseline0001`'s `dist_ratio` (0.479) by a wide margin (0.56-0.71) -- **the original diagnosis (high `approx_kl`/`clip_fraction`, a noisy update process) was correct, but the right response was to train through more of that noise, not suppress it.** The training curve (`experiments/runs/exp0009_longer_baseline/curves.png`) shows a repeating climb-crash-recover oscillation with an upward trend in peak height over ~10M timesteps -- `baseline0001` was simply stopped partway through the first climb, not misconfigured.

**Canonical baseline updated** to `experiments/runs/exp0009_longer_baseline/best_checkpoint_6M.zip` (the checkpoint at 6M timesteps, selected by evaluating the full checkpoint trend rather than trusting the final 10M-step model, which itself scored lower than 6M due to the same oscillation). This is a strict improvement over `baseline0001` on every metric: length 18.6s vs 16.5s, reward 1097 vs 947, velocity error 0.119 vs 0.155 m/s, distance 2.54m vs 1.85m, `dist_ratio` 0.705 vs 0.479, falls 3/20 vs 5/20. Full writeup: `experiments/EXPERIMENT_LOG.md` EXP-0009; decision record: `docs/DECISIONS.md` ADR-004.

The residual failure mode (falls concentrated at the highest commanded speeds -- now seeds 1001/1013/1017, 3/20 instead of 5/20) persists, survives much longer before failing than before, and remains only partially explained (the F4 audit finding that 2/5 of the *original* baseline's falls didn't fit a clean speed-threshold story is now moot, since the new baseline's failures are exactly the 3 highest-speed seeds with no exceptions) -- an honest, smaller remaining limitation, not a blocker.

---

**Second resolution (2026-09-19, canonical baseline `exp0017`, no new training -- re-analysis of existing `eval_summary.json` per-episode data, PROJECT_STATE.md "highest-value next step" #4):** the falls are now fully gone. Re-checking the same three seeds (1001, 1013, 1017 -- commanded speeds 0.415/0.506/0.566 m/s, still the fastest three of the 20 fixed eval seeds) against `exp0017`'s deterministic eval: all three now survive the full 20s (`termination_reason: null`), consistent with the project-wide 0/20 falls already recorded for this checkpoint. The high-speed-command falling failure mode first identified in EXP-0005/F-0002 is resolved -- not by a targeted fix, but as a side effect of the stepping-quality work (EXP-0016/0017).

However, it has not disappeared cleanly -- it has **shifted from falling to curving**. The same 3 fastest-command episodes, while surviving, are the worst 3 of 20 on `distance_ratio` (0.256, 0.360, 0.260 vs. an overall mean of 0.709 and a mean of 0.783 for the other 17 episodes) and have the largest-magnitude yaw drift of the run (+42.5, -65.2, -41.6 deg/10s vs. a 33.95 deg/10s mean-|drift| for the other 17). Mean velocity-tracking error is only mildly worse at high speed (0.069 vs. 0.059 m/s) -- not enough to explain a ~2.7x drop in distance ratio. The likely mechanism: `distance_traveled_m` is straight-line displacement (start to end), and `expected_distance_m` assumes the commanded heading is held; a policy that tracks *speed* reasonably well step-to-step but drifts in *heading* over 20s effectively walks a curve, so its net straight-line progress falls far short of a straight-line expectation even without ever losing balance. This is consistent with the project's independent finding (ADR-010, `heading_deviation_penalty`) that heading control degrades under harder gait demands -- high commanded speed appears to be another condition (alongside longer stride) that stresses heading control specifically, not balance/tracking-speed itself.

**Re-verified against the new canonical (2026-09-19, `exp0026`, no new training -- existing eval data re-analyzed):** the same 3 fastest-command seeds (1001/1013/1017) still survive the full 20s (0/20 falls, unchanged), and the `distance_ratio` degradation at high speed is now much smaller -- 0.870 vs. 0.926 mean for the rest (a ~6% relative drop, vs. `exp0017`'s 63% relative drop in the original finding). Heading still degrades disproportionately at high speed (mean |yaw drift| 42.5 deg/10s vs. 13.2 deg/10s for the rest, a ~3.2x gap, similar in kind to the original finding) -- so the underlying mechanism (high commanded speed stresses heading control specifically) persists, but its impact on actual walking distance is now much smaller thanks to `exp0026`'s generally better tracking. This is incidental evidence, not a targeted fix -- `exp0026` was not trained with high-speed robustness as a goal.

Conclusion: PROJECT_STATE.md's open question ("has the high-speed failure mode been resolved?") is answered -- **yes for falls, no for full tracking quality (though notably improved on the new canonical).** No further action is required to meet the challenge bar (0/20 falls already exceeds it).

**Immediate cheap follow-up (same session, still no new training):** does `exp0019` (best heading-hold checkpoint in the project, per its own controlled straight-line test) also degrade less at high commanded speed? Re-analysis of its existing `eval_summary.json` on the same 3 fastest seeds gives a nuanced, genuinely mixed answer, not a clean "yes": `exp0019`'s *relative* `distance_ratio` drop at high speed is much smaller than canonical's (0.541 vs. 0.644 mean, a 16% relative drop, vs. `exp0017`'s 0.292 vs. 0.783, a 63% relative drop) -- so it holds up distance tracking noticeably better under fast commands. But its *raw* yaw-drift magnitude at those same high-speed seeds is larger, not smaller, than canonical's (mean |yaw drift| 80.1 deg/10s vs. `exp0017`'s 49.8 deg/10s), and the high/low-speed *gap* in yaw drift is much wider for `exp0019` (80.1 vs. 18.0, a ~4.4x ratio) than for `exp0017` (49.8 vs. 34.0, a ~1.5x ratio). So `heading_deviation_penalty` does not simply "fix" high-speed heading in absolute terms -- it appears to decouple heading error from distance-tracking loss (the robot can drift in heading substantially at high speed and still cover close to its usual fraction of commanded distance), rather than preventing the drift itself. This is consistent with what the term was actually calibrated against (a fixed vx=0.3 straight-line test, not the full randomized-command high-speed regime) and is a real limit of that reward term's generalization, not a bug. No new training is warranted from this alone; it is recorded as evidence for whoever next revisits the Pareto surface, so the same untested "speed-specific reward term" idea isn't re-derived from scratch.

### F-0003 — The canonical policy doesn't walk straight and takes tiny, chattery steps, not clean strides
Run(s): `experiments/runs/exp0009_longer_baseline/best_checkpoint_6M.zip` (current canonical baseline), diagnosed via a new tool, `diagnostics/gait_diagnostic.py`, built specifically to check this (a user report that the walk in the video didn't look like real straight-line stepping).

Observed behavior: none of the metrics used so far (episode length, reward, velocity-tracking error, distance traveled) say anything about *heading* or *foot-clearance pattern* -- both were invisible to every prior evaluation. Running the canonical checkpoint for 10s under a clean, fixed forward command (vx=0.3, vy=0.0, no lateral component) and logging base xy/yaw and per-foot height/ground-contact directly:

Quantitative evidence (`diagnostics/reports/gait_diagnostic.png`):
- **Heading**: yaw drifted **95 degrees over 10 seconds** (a near-quarter-turn) with no commanded turning at all. Max lateral deviation from the initial heading direction was 1.35m against only 1.77m of net forward progress -- the trajectory plot shows a curve, not a line: initially fairly straight, then curving hard into what looks like the start of a spiral.
- **Stepping**: each foot lifts off 54-56 times in 10s (~5.5 Hz), airborne only 15-16% of the time each, with foot height oscillating in a narrow 0.030-0.055m band (2.5cm of clearance) that looks, in the height-vs-time and contact-state plots, like rapid high-frequency chatter/vibration rather than deliberate strides with clean stance and swing phases. Both feet are never airborne simultaneously (no leaping), and both are grounded 69% of the time -- so it is not dragging with zero clearance, but the pattern reads as buzzing/shuffling, not walking.

Likely mechanism(s):
1. **Heading drift**: the reward's `tracking_lin_vel` term compares commanded velocity against `base_lin_vel_xy` computed in the pelvis's *own local frame* (`envs/g1_walk_env.py` -> `envs/observations.base_frame_velocity`, `flg_local=True`). This means the policy is rewarded for moving forward *relative to wherever it is currently facing*, never in a fixed world-frame direction -- nothing in the reward penalizes yaw rotation itself. A gait that yaws while walking loses nothing by the reward's own accounting, even though it visibly fails to go straight in the world frame. This is a reward-design gap, not a control or model bug.
2. **Chattery steps**: no term rewards or requires smooth, low-frequency foot motion or a particular stride cadence; `torque_penalty` (quadratic in torque) does not distinguish a few large smooth strides from many small jittery ones if the total squared torque ends up similar. Combined with the noisy, high-`approx_kl` training dynamics already characterized in F-0002, a jittery local minimum plausibly satisfies the tracking reward "well enough" without ever being pushed toward cleaner, larger strides.

Competing explanations: (a) these are genuine reward-design gaps (above) that a general PPO run would exhibit regardless of budget; (b) these are training-budget artifacts that would resolve with (yet) more timesteps, given the oscillating dynamics in F-0002/EXP-0009. (b) is less likely on its own for the *heading* issue specifically -- there is no mechanism by which more training would teach a policy to avoid yaw drift when yaw drift is literally invisible to its reward -- but is plausible as a partial contributor to gait smoothness.

Fastest discriminating test: add the two directly-implicated, minimal reward terms -- an angular-velocity (yaw-rate) penalty targeting (1), and an action-rate penalty targeting (2) -- and rerun training at a comparable budget to EXP-0009 (10M timesteps, since F-0002 established that budget is what a fair comparison needs). If yaw drift and foot-height jitter both improve without regressing `dist_ratio`/fall count, both mechanisms are confirmed.

Result: see EXP-0010 and EXP-0011.

Conclusion (2026-09-18, after EXP-0010/0011): confirmed, and fixed for the heading half of the problem, with one important detour. EXP-0010's first attempt (`ang_vel_penalty=1.0`, chosen by an unverified "same order as `tracking_lin_vel`" heuristic) collapsed the policy to near-total stillness (`dist_ratio` ~0.01) -- caught immediately by the ADR-003 distance-traveled check, the same mechanism that caught EXP-0006's earlier degenerate optimum. Root cause, confirmed by direct measurement rather than another guess: replaying `exp0009`'s actual successful gait through the new reward's math showed its natural yaw wobble cost `-0.44/step` at that weight, exceeding the `+0.80/step` tracking reward it competed with -- standing still was a strictly better deal. Recalibrating to `ang_vel_penalty=0.1` (so the same gait's typical cost is ~5% of the tracking reward, not ~55%) in EXP-0011 fixed it: **yaw drift dropped from 95 to 11.6 degrees per 10 seconds** (88% reduction) in the controlled straight-line test, with no regression in survival, tracking error, distance traveled, or falls. The chattery/small-stride stepping half of F-0003 is only partially addressed (stepping frequency down slightly with `action_rate_penalty=0.01`, clearance essentially unchanged) -- an open item, not claimed as solved.

**Canonical baseline updated again** to `experiments/runs/exp0011_gait_quality_v2/best_checkpoint_10M.zip`. See `docs/DECISIONS.md` ADR-005 and `experiments/EXPERIMENT_LOG.md` EXP-0010/EXP-0011 for full detail.

Follow-up: a larger `action_rate_penalty` or a direct minimum-foot-clearance/stride-length reward term would be the next targeted experiment for the remaining chattery-stepping issue, if pursued further.

### F-0004 — `feet_air_time_bonus` (EXP-0012) got gamed: the policy learned to avoid touchdowns entirely by skating instead of stepping
Run(s): `experiments/runs/exp0012_air_time` (all checkpoints), diagnosed via `diagnostics.gait_diagnostic` and a new foot-slip measurement.

Observed behavior: added a reward for swing duration at the moment of touchdown (`envs/contacts.AirTimeTracker`, weight calibrated by measuring `exp0011`'s actual gait -- see EXP-0012's pre-registration). Result on the controlled straight-line test (vx=0.3, vy=0): liftoff counts **collapsed to 2-21 per 10s** (down from `exp0011`'s 45-47), airborne time **0-5%** (down from 11-14%). The aggregate 20-episode eval still showed a reasonable `dist_ratio` (0.41-0.61 across checkpoints) and even fewer falls (0-1/20) than `exp0011` (2/20) -- by every metric used before F-0003/F-0004, this could have looked like a win. Yaw drift also got dramatically worse on some checkpoints (up to 164 deg/10s) despite `ang_vel_penalty` being unchanged from `exp0011`.

Quantitative evidence: measured foot horizontal velocity *while nominally in contact* directly (finite difference of foot body position, gated on the same contact detector the reward uses). For the `exp0012` checkpoint: mean 0.24-0.26 m/s, 90th percentile ~0.68 m/s, max ~1.1-1.2 m/s -- comparable to the commanded 0.3 m/s forward speed itself. The robot is **sliding its feet along the ground while remaining in geometric contact**, achieving forward progress by skating rather than by lifting and placing its feet.

Likely mechanism: the reward only fires *at the moment of touchdown* (transition from no-contact to contact). A foot that never leaves the ground never triggers that event, so it can never incur the penalty for a too-short swing -- "never touch down" is a *zero-cost* escape from a penalty that was meant to make "touch down too soon" costly. This is a known class of RL reward-hacking failure (the literature's own `feet_air_time` formula, e.g. Rudin et al. 2021, has the identical blind spot; it is usually masked in practice by other terms/curricula this project doesn't have). The lesson from ADR-005 (measure the weight against the *target* gait) was necessary but not sufficient here -- the problem wasn't the weight's magnitude, it was a structural gap in what the reward could see (an event-triggered reward has no way to penalize an event that never happens).

Competing explanations considered: (a) weight `3.0` was simply too aggressive, and a smaller weight would avoid the collapse -- unlikely to be sufficient on its own, since *any* positive weight makes "avoid touchdown" a strictly zero-penalty option that "attempt a full-length swing" is not (attempting a swing risks an early, short, penalized landing if balance requires it); (b) the exploit is structural, not a magnitude problem -- supported by the mechanism above and by checking that stance duration for the skating gait is unbounded (multiple continuous seconds without a liftoff), which no finite weight on a touchdown-only reward can discourage.

Fastest discriminating test: add a complementary term that penalizes continuous stance duration directly (not just rewarding swing duration at touchdown) -- a foot that overstays a maximum stance time accrues a penalty *every step* it continues, which cannot be dodged by avoiding touchdown, unlike the original formulation.

Result: see EXP-0013. **EXP-0013 made things categorically worse (100% fall rate), not better -- see the addendum below.**

Conclusion (2026-09-18, after EXP-0013): the touchdown-avoidance exploit diagnosed above is real and was correctly diagnosed, but closing it with a second, independently-calibrated penalty (`stance_overrun_penalty`) did not produce a working combination -- it produced a much more severe failure (every checkpoint, every episode, 100% falls at ~2.5-3.1s). Traced the mechanism directly (per-step reward-component logging on one checkpoint): the two terms create a narrow, apparently infeasible-at-this-budget corridor. `stance_overrun_penalty` demands a foot lift within ~1.0s; `feet_air_time_bonus` demands, once lifted, a swing of >=0.2s to avoid a penalty. Observed sequence: the policy delays lifting (avoiding a premature short swing), the overrun penalty grows large (-0.8 -> -2.4 -> -4.0 across a few hundred ms), forcing a late, poorly-timed step that *still* doesn't reach the 0.2s swing threshold (triggering the air-time penalty anyway) and visibly destabilizes the robot (pelvis height drops from 0.76m to 0.46m within ~20 control steps immediately after). Both penalties individually measured as "modest" (11% and 7.5% of the tracking reward respectively, calibrated the same measure-first way as every other term in this project) but combined, they do not compose additively -- they fight over the same variable (when to step) with incompatible demands, and neither term's calibration process could have revealed that without actually training the combination.

**This is a second, independent negative result for the feet-air-time reward family, hitting the pre-registered stop condition exactly** ("if liftoff counts don't recover and foot slip doesn't drop, this is a second documented negative result, and it should be abandoned... rather than a third weight-tweaking attempt"). Per that pre-registered discipline: **not pursuing a third attempt at this specific mechanism.** `exp0011_gait_quality_v2` remains the canonical baseline. The chattery-stepping problem (F-0003's second half) remains open and unsolved; two well-instrumented, honestly-documented attempts to fix it (F-0004, this entry) are now on record for whoever continues this work, including the specific mechanism that broke each one -- valuable negative results, not wasted effort.

Follow-up: if pursued again, the next attempt should not be a third weight adjustment on the same two terms -- it would need either (a) a fundamentally different formulation (e.g. reward stride *length*, i.e. foot-to-foot horizontal distance at touchdown, rather than swing *duration*, which doesn't create the same "must lift AND must stay up long enough" double constraint), or (b) training the two terms in sequence via curriculum (stabilize stepping frequency first, only then add the duration requirement) rather than simultaneously from scratch. Neither attempted here; both are reasonable candidates for a future session with a fresh compute budget.

---

**Resolution (2026-09-18, EXP-0016): option (b) above, in a simpler form, worked.** Both EXP-0012 and EXP-0013 trained `feet_air_time_bonus` (and its failed companion) *from scratch*, so the policy could stumble onto "never touch down" before ever discovering real stepping. EXP-0016 instead **warm-started PPO from `exp0011`'s already-good, frequently-stepping checkpoint** and fine-tuned at a reduced learning rate (5e-5 vs. the standard 3e-4, confirmed necessary in a smoke test: `approx_kl` dropped from 0.86 to 0.028 at the lower rate, with the policy's established performance retained immediately rather than shocked) with `feet_air_time_bonus` alone (no `stance_overrun_penalty` -- not even needed this time).

Result, verified the same way F-0004 itself was discovered (direct swing-time measurement, not just aggregate metrics): median swing duration **doubled, 20ms -> 40ms**, airborne time rose to 17-21% (from 11-14%), liftoff frequency held steady (50-51/10s vs. 45-47/10s) -- genuine stepping improvement, not skating (which would show the opposite: fewer liftoffs, less airborne time). Every other metric matched or improved on `exp0011`: velocity-tracking error down 36% (0.134 -> 0.086 m/s), falls down (2/20 -> 1/20), yaw drift down further (42.7 -> 36.9 deg/10s), survival and reward both up. The training curve was also qualitatively different from every from-scratch run in this project -- smooth and steadily improving, without the violent climb-crash-recover oscillation documented in F-0002.

**Canonical baseline updated** to `experiments/runs/exp0016_warmstart_air_time/best_checkpoint_4M.zip`. See `docs/DECISIONS.md` ADR-007 and `experiments/EXPERIMENT_LOG.md` EXP-0016 for full detail. Swing duration, while now 2x better, is still short of a fully deliberate human-scale stride (40ms median vs. a plausible 200-300ms target) -- an honest, smaller residual limitation, with a clear next lever (repeat this same warm-start recipe again, or raise `feet_air_time_threshold_s`) if pursued further.

**Second update (2026-09-18, EXP-0017):** repeated the same warm-start recipe again (fine-tuned from `exp0016`'s own checkpoint, same config/learning rate, 10M more timesteps) to test whether swing duration would keep increasing. Result: **swing duration plateaued** (median stayed 40ms; mean rose from 35.0ms to 47.6ms as the distribution tightened into a 40-60ms band, but the ceiling didn't move), while every other metric kept improving substantially -- `dist_ratio` 0.627 -> 0.709, velocity error 0.086 -> 0.061 m/s, and **0/20 falls** (the first perfect score of the project). This separates two things EXP-0016 alone couldn't distinguish: continued training kept refining tracking precision and gait consistency around the established (still-short) stride, but did not, on its own, push stride duration past ~40-60ms. **Canonical baseline updated again** to `experiments/runs/exp0017_warmstart_air_time_long/best_checkpoint_8M.zip` -- see `docs/DECISIONS.md` ADR-008. The clear next lever for a longer, more human-like stride specifically: raise `feet_air_time_threshold_s` (e.g. to 0.3-0.4s) and repeat the fine-tune, since the current threshold (0.2s) is no longer the binding constraint on further improvement -- untested, left for whoever continues this work.

**Third update (2026-09-18, EXP-0018):** tested the next lever -- doubling `feet_air_time_bonus` (3.0 -> 6.0, measured first against `exp0017`'s own gait) rather than raising the threshold, since it's the more conservative change. It worked as intended -- median swing duration rose further to 60ms, tripling the original 20ms baseline -- but revealed a genuine **Pareto trade-off, not another clean win**: `dist_ratio` dropped 23% relative (0.709 -> 0.548) and the controlled straight-line diagnostic showed heading control measurably worse at every checkpoint checked (11.6 -> 28-65 deg/10s), including a first-ever brief "both feet airborne" moment at the 8M checkpoint. **Not promoted** -- `exp0017` remains canonical; `exp0018`'s checkpoint is kept as a documented alternative for anyone who prioritizes visible stride length over tracking/heading precision (see `docs/DECISIONS.md` ADR-009).

**Fourth update / final conclusion (2026-09-19, EXP-0019/0020):** rather than accept the ADR-009 trade-off, measured *why* `ang_vel_penalty` didn't prevent it: replaying `exp0017` (good heading) and `exp0018` (bad heading) through the same controlled test showed both have similar mean instantaneous rotation-rate-squared, but very different mean heading-*deviation*-squared (0.038 vs. 0.112) -- `ang_vel_penalty` can't distinguish a small steady drift bias from a larger but zero-mean oscillation, because it only sees magnitude, not direction persistence. Added `heading_deviation_penalty` (penalizes deviation from the heading at episode start directly) and fine-tuned from `exp0018`'s longer-stride checkpoint (EXP-0019). Result: the best heading of any checkpoint in this project (-7.5 deg/10s on the controlled test, better than `exp0017`'s own -19.0 deg) while matching `exp0018`'s 60ms median swing duration exactly -- the mechanism works and is better-targeted than `ang_vel_penalty` alone. A follow-up (EXP-0020, more budget on the same lineage) confirmed this specific configuration has plateaued, not simply undertrained (dist_ratio oscillated 0.52-0.63 without a clear trend, yaw drift got worse at later checkpoints).

However, `exp0019` does not fully match `exp0017` on tracking precision (`dist_ratio` 0.613 vs. 0.709, `vel_err` 0.093 vs. 0.061) -- **the final state of this entire investigation (EXP-0010 through EXP-0020) is a documented 3-way Pareto surface, not a single dominant checkpoint.** `exp0017_warmstart_air_time_long` remains canonical (best tracking precision, 0/20 falls); `exp0018_air_time_v3` (longest stride) and `exp0019_heading_hold` (best heading + long stride, weaker tracking) are both kept as documented alternatives for different priorities -- see `docs/DECISIONS.md` ADR-010. The chattery-stepping-and-heading investigation is considered concluded for this project: swing duration tripled from the original baseline, heading drift was independently improved past the original fix's own result, and the remaining tension between these properties and tracking precision is honestly characterized rather than hidden or arbitrarily declared solved.

### F-0005 -- The canonical spins in place at commanded velocity zero, undetected until directly measured
Run(s): `exp0038_clock_heading` (`experiments/runs/exp0038_clock_heading/best_checkpoint_5M.zip`), 15 seeds forced to command (0, 0).

Observed behavior: forced to a zero command (never sampled during training -- `vx ~ U[0.0, 0.6]` makes exactly 0 a measure-zero event), the canonical does not fall and drifts very little in position (0.24-0.30 m over 20 s), but rotates in a consistent direction: 15/15 seeds gave +19.2 to +26.2 degrees over 20 s (mean +22.5, std 2.1) -- never near zero, never negative. A shorter, separate 4 s measurement gave the same sign (mean yaw rate +1.84 deg/s). This went undetected through every prior evaluation because none of them command exactly zero: the standard 20-seed eval samples `vx` continuously above 0, and the controlled straight-line diagnostic always commands `vx=0.3`.

Likely mechanism: the left and right feet are not mechanically symmetric in the learned gait -- left foot z while standing: mean 0.0392 m, max 0.0639 m; right: mean 0.0380 m, max 0.0556 m (the left foot lifts higher). Yaw rate correlates weakly with (left_z - right_z) (r = 0.257): turning is *associated* with the left foot swinging, consistent with an uneven push-off, but this correlation is not strong enough to be the whole story. The gait clock's own asymmetry (left phase leads, right lags by half a cycle -- `envs/gait.py`) is a plausible root cause of an asymmetric learned gait, though not directly tested here.

Why `heading_deviation_penalty` (already in the canonical's reward, weight 0.25) does not suppress this: measured directly, its raw cost at zero command is only 0.0715 rad^2/step -- smaller than the low-speed-band cost (0.106) that the same weight already controls well during normal walking (R-0006). At weight 0.25 this is 0.0179/step, 0.4% of the policy's ~4.18/step total reward: far too weak a signal at zero command specifically, where velocity tracking (already near-maximal regardless of small heading error) supplies little competing pressure toward straightness.

Conclusion: a real, previously-uncaught defect, found only because the canonical was deliberately tested at a command it was never trained to receive exactly. Not fatal (no fall, minimal position drift) but real: a user commanding "stop" would see the robot turn about 20-25 degrees over 20 seconds.

**Resolution (2026-09-22, EXP-0041):** raising `heading_deviation_penalty` from 0.25 to 1.0 and fine-tuning 1M timesteps onto the canonical reduced the zero-command spin to 9.6-9.7 deg/20s (a 56-57% reduction, confirmed on both the original and 30 fresh seeds) while matching or slightly exceeding the canonical on every other tracked metric across 225 evaluation episodes with zero falls -- promoted to canonical, ADR-017. A genuinely important caveat surfaced during this fix: the effect is **not durable under continued training** -- checked across the full 6M-step run, the spin re-grows steadily with more fine-tuning (12.6 at 2M, 15.1 at 4M, 19.9 at 6M, almost back to the original 22.5), because zero command is never sampled during training, so a general reward-weight increase only transiently affects behavior there; continued optimization on the actual (non-zero-command) training distribution does not reinforce it. The promoted checkpoint is a favorable point passed through during fine-tuning, not a structurally guaranteed fix -- any future fine-tune built on it should re-check the zero-command test, since further training could erode it again the same way. A more durable fix would need zero or near-zero commands actually represented in the training distribution, not just a heavier general penalty; untried.

**Second resolution (2026-09-22, EXP-0042): the untried idea above was tried.** Added `command.zero_command_prob` (`config/loader.py`, `envs/reset.sample_command`) so exact (0,0) commands are actually sampled during training (15% of episodes), and fine-tuned 6M more steps from `exp0041`'s 1M checkpoint. The result was not a clean fix -- the checkpoint trend was turbulent for 4 of 6 checkpoints (1M: 21.4, 2M: 15.1, 3M: 18.4, 4M: 14.9 deg/20s, all *worse* than the pre-registered 12 deg/20s bar, and the 1M point briefly worse than doing nothing) -- but the run's final two checkpoints, 5M (5.1) and 6M (8.7), are both its best, a different and more encouraging shape than EXP-0041's trend (which was worst at its own endpoint). The 5M checkpoint, verified on seeds disjoint from selection, beats `exp0041`@1M head-to-head on the identical 100-seed validation protocol: zero-command drift 5.1-7.0 vs 9.6-9.7 deg/20s (30-45% lower), general mixed-command yaw drift 9.99 vs 12.96 deg/10s (23% lower -- the direct-training approach improved heading discipline beyond just the exact zero-command case), `dist_ratio` tied (0.975 vs 0.979), falls tied (0/100 both) -- promoted to canonical, ADR-018.

**F-0005 is now better-mitigated than at any prior point (zero-command drift roughly a quarter of the original ~22.5 deg/20s), but is not declared closed.** Durability past 6M of *this* fix is unconfirmed -- the mechanism (the signal is present in every future training batch, not a static weight) gives real reason to expect it holds better than EXP-0041's, but only two data points at the tail of one noisy run support that expectation so far. A follow-up that continues fine-tuning further (e.g. to 10-12M) and re-runs this exact zero-command test would directly answer whether `zero_command_prob` training is structurally self-reinforcing or just found another favorable point to pass through.

**Third resolution (2026-09-23, EXP-0043): the follow-up above was run, and answered the question.** Continued the exact same `exp0042` training trajectory 6M more steps (to 12M cumulative) and re-ran the zero-command test at every new checkpoint. Result: all 6 new checkpoints (7M-12M) cleared the 12 deg/20s bar that 4 of `exp0042`'s own 6 checkpoints had missed, and the trend was **net-improving, not flat-with-noise** -- 11.8 (7M) down to **2.1 deg/20s at 12M**, confirmed on 30 fresh seeds (1.7), the lowest zero-command spin measured anywhere in this project. General mixed-command yaw drift also improved further (8.33 vs `exp0042`'s 9.99 deg/10s on the 100-seed validation set), `dist_ratio` stayed tied (0.974 vs 0.975), and falls stayed at 0/100 -- promoted to canonical, ADR-019.

**F-0005 is now considered well-resolved.** Twelve million cumulative steps of consistent, non-eroding, actively-improving behavior on the exact metric that failed under two earlier fixes (a static reward weight, then an under-tested distribution change) is substantially stronger evidence than either prior checkpoint had. This is deliberately not phrased as "permanently, unconditionally fixed" -- this project has more than once found a trend reverse only after further training stages the original evidence didn't cover -- but the honest, current statement is that the defect first found in F-0005 is fixed and the fix has now been stress-tested for durability, not just proposed. Any future fine-tune built on this checkpoint should still re-run the zero-command test before assuming the property transfers, as standing practice, not because a specific new erosion risk is expected.

### F-0006 — The canonical falls on more than half of pure-lateral-command episodes, undetected until a command-space grid was actually built
Run(s): `exp0043_zero_command_durability_check` @ 12M (current canonical), `evaluation/evaluate_gait_quality.py`'s command grid, 20 seeds (1000-1019), command `(vx, vy) = (0.0, 0.18)`.

Observed behavior: every prior evaluation set (standard 20-seed, banded 60/100-seed, the F-0005 zero-command tests) samples `vx` continuously from a distribution centered on forward walking, with `vy` a secondary, usually-small component. Forcing a pure-lateral command near the top of the trained range (`vy_range = [-0.2, 0.2]`) -- something never done in any prior evaluation in this project -- gives **11/20 falls (55%)**, a rate wildly out of line with every other bucket tested (0/20 at stand-still, slow, medium, high forward, and diagonal commands, all evaluated in the same run with the same tooling). This is exactly the class of gap the gait-quality program's command-space grid (`docs/GAIT_QUALITY_OBJECTIVE.md`) was built to catch, and it caught one on its first run -- the same shape of discovery as F-0005 (a real, previously-invisible defect found only because a specific untested command region was deliberately probed).

Quantitative evidence (gait-quality metrics, same run): the lateral bucket is an outlier on nearly every symmetry/geometry metric simultaneously, not just falls -- `double_support_fraction` 0.554 (vs. 0.34-0.42 elsewhere), `duty_factor_left` 0.899 (vs. 0.63-0.72 elsewhere) while `duty_factor_right` stays in the normal 0.67-0.71 range, `swing_duration_mean_asym` -0.298 (roughly 4-7x every other bucket's asymmetry, which ranges -0.04 to -0.09), and `torque_rms_asym` 0.156 (vs. 0.05-0.13 elsewhere). In plain terms: under a pure sideways command, the left leg all but stops stepping (locks in stance ~90% of the cycle) while the right leg keeps a roughly normal duty cycle -- a strongly asymmetric, one-legged-support gait attempting to shuffle sideways, not a designed lateral gait.

Likely mechanism(s): (1) the training command distribution samples `vy` uniformly in `[-0.2, 0.2]` *independently* of `vx ~ U[0.0, 0.6]`, so a command near `(0, 0.2)` -- forward speed near zero, lateral speed near max -- is a real but low-density region of the training distribution (both marginals allow it, but their product puts little mass exactly there relative to the much more common "mostly-forward, small lateral" combination); (2) the gait clock and every per-step gait reward term (`gait_contact_bonus`, `feet_swing_height_penalty`, `contact_foot_vel_penalty`) were calibrated and have only ever been checked against forward-walking gaits (R-0004 through R-0006, every controlled diagnostic in this project's history commands `vx=0.3, vy=0`) -- there is no evidence the learned stepping pattern generalizes to a sideways-dominant gait, and the strong left/right asymmetry specifically under this command suggests it does not.

Competing explanations: (a) pure low-density-region undertraining (the policy simply hasn't seen enough of this command corner to learn it, and more/better-distributed command sampling would fix it); (b) a structural mismatch between the fixed-period, forward-oriented gait clock and what a sideways gait actually requires (a gait clock built by watching forward walking may encode assumptions -- e.g. which leg leads, how weight shifts -- that are wrong for lateral motion); (c) some combination, with (b) more consistent with the severity and one-sidedness of the asymmetry (undertraining alone would more plausibly produce noisy, not consistently one-legged, failure).

Fastest discriminating test: not yet run. The cheapest discriminator between (a) and (b): evaluate an *older* checkpoint from earlier in the lineage (e.g. `exp0038`, before the gait clock's per-step terms were as dominant, or `exp0026`, pre-clock entirely) on the identical lateral-command grid. If the pre-clock checkpoint falls *less* on pure lateral commands, that implicates the clock/gait-reward machinery specifically (favors (b)); if it falls similarly or worse, undertraining/command-distribution density (a) is favored instead.

Conclusion: a real, previously-uncaught, severe defect in an in-range command region, discovered by the same discipline that found F-0005 -- test the command grid deliberately, don't trust aggregate/forward-biased sampling to surface every failure mode. Not yet root-caused or fixed.

Follow-up: this is flagged as a high-priority open item but is **explicitly out of scope for this session's core fluidity/efficiency/symmetry experiment families**, which focus on the well-trained forward-walking regime per the task's own framing. It is recorded here, with the discriminating test above pre-specified, so it is not lost and can be picked up directly (by this session if time allows after the core program, or by a future one) without re-deriving the finding. Per `docs/GAIT_QUALITY_OBJECTIVE.md`'s regression gates, any future candidate is checked against this bucket too, so a fluidity/efficiency change that happens to make this worse will be caught, even though fixing it is not this program's primary target.

**Unplanned resolution (2026-09-24, incidental to EXP-0047): substantial improvement, confirmed on fresh seeds, but not a complete fix.** The full 20-seed gait-quality rebaseline on the new canonical (`exp0047_ankle_gain_more_zerocmd`, ADR-020) re-ran this exact bucket on the identical 20 seeds (1000-1019) that produced the original 11/20 finding -- and got **0/20 falls**. This was not the target of EXP-0047 (which was designed for F-0007's swing-duration question, on forward-walking buckets only) and was not checked for during that experiment's own evaluation; it surfaced only when the routine post-promotion rebaseline covered the full command grid again. A follow-up check on a fresh, disjoint 60-seed set (2000-2059, never used in this bucket's original discovery or in this rebaseline) found **6/60 falls (10%)** -- a real, large improvement over the original 55% rate (an ~82% relative reduction), but **not the complete fix the 20-seed result alone suggested**. This is exactly the kind of seed-dependent overclaim this project has been burned by before (ADR-014's own motivation: "comparisons that looked decisive on the 20 standard seeds changed on fresh ones") -- the original 20 seeds happened to land in the now-improved region more consistently than the broader distribution does.

The underlying asymmetric pattern this entry originally diagnosed is still present and was re-measured (`duty_factor_left` 0.84 vs `duty_factor_right` 0.67, `swing_duration_mean_asym` -0.19, both similar in shape to the original finding) -- the gait under a pure lateral command is still a strongly asymmetric, left-leg-heavy pattern, not a redesigned symmetric one. What changed is that the robot survives that asymmetric pattern much more often than before, consistent with the ankle-gain increase and/or the more heavily-trained low-command regime (`zero_command_prob` 0.30) providing additional stability margin -- enough to matter a great deal (55% -> 10%), not enough to fully close the gap.

**Conclusion: F-0006 is substantially improved (not closed).** Falls under a pure-lateral command dropped from 11/20 (55%) to 6/60 (10%), confirmed on seeds disjoint from discovery. This was incidental to a fix aimed at a different problem (F-0007), and the underlying asymmetric mechanism remains unaddressed -- a real, still-open target for anyone who picks this up, now at a substantially lower baseline than when first found. Not re-derived from a controlled mechanism test; a genuinely targeted fix (rather than an incidental side effect of an unrelated change) remains untried.

### F-0007 — The realized swing phase is far shorter than the gait clock schedules, and duty factor exceeds the configured stance fraction at every tested speed
Run(s): `exp0043_zero_command_durability_check` @ 12M (current canonical), `evaluation/evaluate_gait_quality.py` baseline run (seeds 1000-1019, all forward-walking buckets), plus a standalone, untrained kinematic probe (no policy involved).

Observed behavior: the gait clock (`envs/gait.py`) schedules `stance_fraction=0.55` of each 0.8s cycle in stance, i.e. a **360ms** swing window per foot. Measured realized swing duration across the whole speed range is **75-121ms** -- roughly a third of the scheduled window -- and duty factor (stance time / full cycle) is **0.63-0.90**, well above the clock's own 0.55, at every commanded speed from stand-still through the fastest trained command. Clock *adherence* (a per-step binary match, reported at ~81-82% in prior evaluations) does not catch this, because a foot that leaves the ground briefly and re-plants well before the scheduled swing window ends can still mostly satisfy a per-instant "is this foot doing roughly what's scheduled" check.

Quantitative evidence: peak foot clearance (0.049-0.065m across buckets) also consistently undershoots `feet_swing_height_penalty`'s own target height (0.08m) by 20-40%. A direct correlation check across 1206 individual swings (10 episodes, medium-speed command): swing duration and peak achieved foot height correlate at **r=0.68** (swings binned by duration: <80ms swings average 0.039m peak height; 120-160ms swings average 0.068m, closer to target) -- longer swings reach measurably higher, consistent with clearance being *limited by available time*, not chosen independently of it. A standalone kinematic probe (no trained policy, no reward involved -- a scripted PD step-response test: command a single joint's PD target to jump by the maximum single-control-step excursion the policy can ever request, 0.25 rad, and measure convergence time under the exact PD gains/decimation this project uses) found the **knee** (the highest-gain joint, kp=150) needs ~72ms to reach 90% of that step, while **ankle_pitch** (kp=40, the lowest-gain leg joint) and **hip_pitch** (kp=100, but higher effective inertia) do not converge to 90% within 200ms at all.

Likely mechanism(s): given that (1) targets are reissued every control step (20ms, not held for a full swing), (2) the lowest-gain joint most directly responsible for foot lift (`ankle_pitch`) is also the slowest to converge to any given target, and (3) realized swing duration (75-121ms) is on the same order as -- not far above -- the convergence times measured in (isolated, simplified) probe (2), a **control-rate limitation is at least a plausible partial cause**: even if the policy "wanted" a longer, higher swing, the combination of low ankle gain and per-step target reissue may make a slow, deliberate swing trajectory harder to execute cleanly than a fast, low one within the available window. This does not rule out a reward-side contribution too -- `feet_air_time_bonus` (weight 3.0, threshold 0.2s) is *currently a net penalty* at these swing durations (raw = duration - threshold < 0 whenever duration < 200ms, true for essentially every swing measured), which is correctly signed to push toward longer swings but may simply be too weak relative to whatever advantage (torque savings, stability, faster resynchronization with the gait-contact-bonus schedule) a short swing offers.

Competing explanations: (a) purely control-rate-limited (raising ankle PD gains would let the same reward produce longer/higher swings); (b) purely reward-limited (the policy could execute a longer swing under current gains but the net incentive doesn't favor it); (c) both, interacting -- a control limitation raises the *cost* of attempting a long swing (harder to land cleanly, more torque, more fall risk), which a reward only weakly favoring long swings then fails to overcome. (c) is the most consistent single story given the isolated PD probe shows a real, sizeable rate limit at exactly the joint that matters most, while the reward evidence shows a correctly-signed but plausibly-underpowered term at the same time -- but this has not been decisively distinguished from (a) or (b) alone.

Fastest discriminating test: **not yet run as a trained comparison** (the PD probe above is a cheap, untrained proxy, not a substitute). The cheapest actual discriminator: fine-tune a small, isolated ankle-gain increase (`ankle_pitch`/`ankle_roll` kp raised modestly, kd adjusted to keep damping ratio comparable) from the current canonical at low LR, and check whether swing duration/peak clearance move toward the clock's schedule *without* any reward change -- if they do, (a)/(c) is supported and control tuning alone helps; if they don't move, (b) is favored and the next lever should be reward-side (e.g. raising `feet_air_time_bonus`'s weight, recalibrated against its now-measured near-zero net effect at current swing durations).

Conclusion: the short, choppy character of the current gait is not fully explained by any single previously-documented cause. This entry consolidates fresh, first-party measurement (not assumption) pointing at a plausible, testable, partly-control-level mechanism, as instructed by this program's Family 1 ("profile before touching rewards; consider control changes, not only reward changes").

Follow-up: EXP-0045 (see `experiments/EXPERIMENT_LOG.md`) runs the discriminating test above.

**Resolution attempt 1 (2026-09-23, EXP-0045): partial, asymmetric support, with an important complication.** Raising `ankle_pitch`/`ankle_roll` kp 40->60 (reward unchanged) increased the right foot's medium-bucket swing duration by 13.9% (115 -> 131ms) at the checkpoint closest to the target -- real support for the control-rate hypothesis, at least for that leg. The **left foot barely responded at all** (103 -> 106ms, +2.9%) despite the identical relative gain change -- evidence the left foot's shorter swing has a different, not-purely-control-rate cause, still unidentified. Separately and unpredicted: the gain change destabilized the zero-command fix (F-0005) non-monotonically across checkpoints (7.8, 2.7, 8.1, 8.5 deg/20s at 1M-4M respectively), failing the hard regression gate (5.1 deg/20s ceiling) at 3 of 4 checkpoints including the one that best matched the swing-duration target. **Not promoted** -- no checkpoint cleared both bars simultaneously. Kept as a documented negative-leaning result: it rules out "ankle gain alone" as a clean fix and surfaces a new general risk (a control-mechanics change can silently destabilize a low-density-training-input behavior even while every forward-walking metric improves). See `experiments/EXPERIMENT_LOG.md` EXP-0045 for full numbers.

Follow-up 2: EXP-0046 tests the reward-side lever instead (raising `feet_air_time_bonus`'s weight, unchanged PD gains, avoiding EXP-0045's zero-command confound entirely).

**Resolution attempt 2 (2026-09-23, EXP-0046): the reward-side lever alone does not move swing duration, but cleanly confirms EXP-0045's mechanism.** Raising `feet_air_time_bonus` 3.0 -> 5.0 (PD gains unchanged) produced essentially no swing-duration change on either foot at any checkpoint (best case +8.7% on the right foot at one checkpoint, left foot flat or slightly down at all four) -- a clear miss, in contrast to the same term's much larger effect (median swing roughly tripled) when raised on an earlier, pre-gait-clock lineage in EXP-0018. The likely explanation: the current reward's per-step gait-clock terms create a much stronger pull toward the present short-swing timing than the simpler reward EXP-0018 tested against, which a touchdown-only bonus of this magnitude does not overcome. **Zero-command drift stayed stable and well within the hard gate at every checkpoint (2.0-4.0 deg/20s)** -- directly confirming EXP-0045's destabilization was specific to the control-mechanics (PD gain) change, not a generic fine-tuning risk. Not promoted (primary hypothesis failed). See `experiments/EXPERIMENT_LOG.md` EXP-0046.

**Combined picture after two single-lever attempts:** the right foot responds to a control change (EXP-0045) but not a reward change (EXP-0046) -- favoring a real physical/kinematic component to its limitation, consistent with F-0007's own PD-response-time probe. The left foot responds to neither -- a distinct, still-unresolved question (candidate mechanisms include the gait clock's own phase-lead asymmetry, since the clock's `clock_features` is built from the left foot's phase specifically, with the right foot always following at a fixed offset -- untested). Follow-up 3 (EXP-0047) combines the partially-working control lever with a direct countermeasure for its zero-command side effect, rather than repeating either single-lever change again.

**Resolution (2026-09-23, EXP-0047): the combined lever works, and cleanly.** Ankle-gain increase (as EXP-0045) plus doubled `command.zero_command_prob` (0.15 -> 0.30, direct countermeasure for EXP-0045's specific failure mode) produced right-foot swing duration of 134-139ms at the 3M/4M checkpoints (+16.5% to +20.9% over baseline, exceeding EXP-0045's own best result) **and** zero-command drift that was stable and monotonically *improving* across every checkpoint (2.6 -> 2.4 -> 2.2 -> 2.0 deg/20s) -- the best zero-command result recorded anywhere in this project, not just an avoided regression. The left foot moved too, for the first time (up to +3.9%), more than either single-lever experiment alone, though it still trails the right foot substantially -- narrowed, not closed. Every task-performance metric matched or beat the canonical. **Promoted to canonical, ADR-020**, superseding `exp0043`.

F-0007 is now considered **substantially improved** (right-foot swing duration up ~20%, closer to but still well short of the gait clock's own 360ms schedule) but **not fully resolved**: the left foot's disproportionately smaller response to every lever tried so far (control, reward, and their combination) is real, reproducible across three experiments, and still unexplained. The leading untested hypothesis is structural rather than magnitude-based: the gait clock's `clock_features` (`envs/gait.py`) is built from the left foot's phase specifically, with the right foot always following at a fixed offset -- this asymmetric role in the clock's own definition is a plausible, previously-unconsidered root cause for why the two feet respond differently to levers that were applied symmetrically. Untested.

**Further investigation (2026-09-24, EXP-0048): the left foot's ceiling IS control-rate-limited, ruling out the strong "structurally capped" hypothesis -- but the fix has a severe side effect.** Before training anything, direct model inspection confirmed left/right physical symmetry (body mass, inertia, actuator forcerange, and every sagittal-plane joint range identical between legs -- the one asymmetric joint range, hip_roll, is a correctly mirrored convention necessary for a symmetric gait, not a bug, and doesn't affect vertical foot lift). A per-swing ceiling measurement (20 episodes, canonical) found something the earlier mean-based comparisons hadn't shown: the left foot's swing duration has a hard ceiling, never once exceeding 140ms across 1127 individual swings, while the right foot regularly reaches 180ms across 1080 swings -- a real, reproducible cap, not just a lower average. New per-leg PD gain support (`config.pd_gains_left`, `control/pd_controller.build_asymmetric_gains`) let this be tested directly: raising the left ankle's gain further than the right's (kp 60->90) **moved the ceiling to 180ms -- matching the right foot's own historical ceiling for the first time** -- and brought mean left/right swing duration to near parity (122/124ms, vs. the canonical's 106/135ms). This is a decisive result against the strongest form of the structural hypothesis: the left foot's shorter swing is not an immovable cap independent of control authority.

**But this specific fix is not usable.** The same checkpoints show yaw drift 2.5-3.5x the established soft gate (19.6-27.7 vs. 10.8 deg/10s) and `dist_ratio` down to 0.81-0.85 (hard gate: >=0.94) -- a severe, consistent regression across both checkpoints checked, not a fluke or a close call. An asymmetric PD gain appears to introduce a systematic left/right timing imbalance that the existing heading-control reward terms (calibrated, throughout this project's entire history, only against symmetric-leg dynamics) do not counteract. **Not promoted.**

**Current state of F-0007:** the left foot's swing-duration limitation has two established components now, not one -- it is genuinely control-rate-limited (confirmed by direct counter-example: more authority measurably raises its ceiling), but naively supplying that authority asymmetrically destabilizes heading control badly enough to be unusable. The gait clock's left-phase-leads structural convention remains a plausible explanation for a different, narrower question: why did the *symmetric*-gain-trained policy never discover this capability on its own, even though EXP-0048 shows it is physically reachable? That question is untested. Two concrete next steps, neither attempted this session: (1) a smaller, graded asymmetric-gain step to check whether the heading-control cost scales smoothly or is a step-function threshold; (2) recalibrate the heading-control reward terms specifically for an asymmetric-gain regime, rather than assuming they generalize for free from a regime that never had one.

**Follow-up (2026-09-24, EXP-0049): tested (1) above -- the two costs/benefits of the asymmetric-gain lever do not move together, ruling out a simple gain-magnitude sweep as the fix.** At half the gain step (left kp=75, vs. EXP-0048's 90 and the canonical's 60), the heading-control cost scaled down substantially and predictably (yaw 10.0 vs. EXP-0048's 19.6-24.1 deg/10s, close to the soft gate rather than 2.5-3.5x over it) -- but the swing-duration benefit essentially vanished too (102-107ms across all 4 checkpoints, statistically flat vs. the canonical's 106ms), rather than shrinking proportionally. **Heading cost behaves roughly continuously in the gain step; the swing-duration benefit behaves like a threshold effect**, present near kp=90 and essentially absent at kp=75 -- the policy appears to need something close to the full EXP-0048 step before it changes left-foot strategy at all. This rules out "search for a sweet-spot gain value between 60 and 90" as a promising plan, since no tested point captures a meaningful fraction of the benefit at an acceptable cost. Not promoted (fails the swing-duration criterion outright). A separate, unexplained observation: lateral-command falls were *worse* at the smaller step (3-5/5 on a 5-seed pilot) than at the larger one (0-1/5) -- opposite the direction a smooth relationship would predict, though on a small enough sample to not be treated as confirmed.

**This closes the "asymmetric ankle gain" experimental line for this session.** F-0007's remaining open questions -- recalibrating heading-control rewards for an asymmetric-gain regime specifically, and the still-untested clock-structure hypothesis for why the symmetric-trained policy never found this capability on its own -- are recommended next steps, not attempted here.
